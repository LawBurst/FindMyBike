How geometry affects your rideFit · climbing · handling

Start with how a bike should feel. These measurements work together, so no single number tells the whole story.

Fit & comfort

Reach measures the horizontal distance from the crank axle (bottom bracket) to the top of the head tube. Longer reach: more room to move while standing; shorter reach: a more compact position. Handlebar position also depends on stem and bars.

Stack measures the vertical distance between those same points. Higher stack: usually a taller, more upright front end; lower stack: a lower, more forward-leaning position.

Effective top tube influences how stretched out you feel seated. Standover height measures from the ground up to the top tube: lower means more clearance between the frame and your body when standing over it.

Climbing

Seat tube angle describes the direction of the seated-position line. A steeper angle (78° vs. 75°) generally places your hips farther forward, which can make it easier to keep the front wheel down on steep climbs. A slacker angle places you farther back; comfort and traction depend on your fit and the terrain.

Chainstay length is roughly the distance from the crank axle to the rear-wheel axle. Longer chainstays: generally improve front-wheel contact on steep climbs and add stability. Shorter chainstays: make it easier to lift the front wheel and steer through tight turns, but can require more weight forward on steep climbs.

Descending stability

Head tube angle measures the steering axis relative to level ground. A smaller angle (63° instead of 67°) is called slacker: the fork leans farther back at the top and steering often feels calmer on steep, fast terrain. A larger, steeper angle generally gives quicker steering response at slower speeds.

Wheelbase is the distance between the two wheel axles. Longer: often feels steadier at speed; shorter: often changes direction more easily. The whole bike's geometry, tires and suspension influence the result.

The green fork ends at the center of the front wheel (the axle). Dashed lines extend the steering axis to level ground, where the head angle is measured. The 63° fork leans farther rearward at the top than the 67° fork. Fork offset and wheel components are simplified for clarity.

Agility & cornering

Shorter wheelbase: generally easier to maneuver through tight turns and switchbacks. Longer wheelbase: generally more stable at speed, but may need more space and rider input to turn.

Shorter chainstays often make it easier to lift the front wheel, manual or pivot the bike. Longer chainstays can help distribute weight between the wheels and feel more balanced in fast corners.

Lower bottom bracket: can feel more settled when cornering; higher: adds obstacle clearance. Neither is automatically better.

Pedal clearance

Bottom bracket height is the crank-axle height above the ground. Higher: more room for pedals to clear rocks and roots, although the bike may feel less planted. Lower: often feels more stable in turns, but pedals are more likely to strike obstacles.

Actual clearance also depends on crank length, tire size and suspension sag (how far the suspension compresses under your weight). Learn to pause or level your pedals over obstacles.

Dropper-post fit

A dropper post lowers the saddle using a handlebar lever, giving you space to move on descents. Longer dropper travel lets the saddle move farther down, provided the frame and rider fit allow it.

Shorter seat tube: often makes it easier to fit a long-travel dropper and lower the saddle fully. Longer seat tube: may limit saddle drop, especially for shorter-legged riders. Insertion depth (how far the post fits inside the frame) matters just as much as seat tube length.

These are useful tendencies, not guarantees. Suspension setup, geometry setting, fit and riding style can change how a bike feels.

FINDMYBIKE / eMTB EDITORIAL

The Future of eMTB

Is the next generation worth waiting for? Why integrated eCVT drivetrains may reshape trail mountain biking.

THE TECHNOLOGY

What is eCVT, and why does it matter?

eCVT means electronically controlled continuously variable transmission. Instead of choosing between 12 or so fixed gears, the drivetrain can smoothly select any ratio between its lowest and highest limits. More importantly, combining the transmission with an intelligent electric motor opens up very different ways to ride.

A typical eMTB still uses a rear cassette (multiple sprockets), a derailleur that moves the chain between them, and separate motor assistance. An integrated motor + eCVT manages the gearing inside the drive unit, eliminating the rear derailleur and multi-speed cassette. It can drive the wheel with a simple chain or, on compatible frames, a belt.

Choose how you want to ride

1. Set your pedaling cadence

Prefer to pedal at 80 RPM? The transmission automatically adjusts its ratio as the terrain changes to help keep your legs turning at that rhythm, within the system's limits. You don't need to choose an easier or harder gear.

2. Set your own effort

Want to contribute roughly 150 watts yourself? A rider-power-target mode can coordinate transmission ratio and electric assistance to help keep your effort near that target while conditions change. The motor supplies more or less assistance as needed, subject to battery, power, traction and control limits. This is a capability reported for emerging systems, not a guarantee on every eCVT.

3. Shift virtual gears yourself

Prefer familiar shifting? The software can create simulated gear steps that you select using handlebar controls. It feels like changing gears, but no derailleur moves a chain across a cassette. Some designs also offer automatic shifting between virtual gears.

One drivetrain, multiple ways to ride. With eCVT, you can choose your preferred pedaling cadence, set how much power you want to contribute, or shift manually through virtual gears.

Important distinction: eCVT controls gear ratio, while the motor controller regulates assistance. Maintaining a rider-wattage target requires those controls to work together. Ordinary automatic shifting can also exist with fixed gears; continuous ratio control is what makes eCVT different. See the Avinox MG reporting and Gobao X1P reporting for the proposed modes.

THE PREDICTION

The Next Big Leap in Electric Mountain Biking

Simpler drivetrains. Smarter shifting. Better handling. Less maintenance.

Electric mountain bikes are approaching a significant technological shift. Rather than simply adding more motor power or battery capacity, the next generation of integrated eCVT systems changes how power is delivered, how the bike handles, and how much maintenance the drivetrain requires.

The prediction: eCVT will eventually become the dominant drivetrain technology in electric mountain biking, with 2027 marking the beginning of that transition. For trail and all-mountain riding, the appeal goes far beyond automatic shifting.

  • Better suspension response. Removing the rear cassette and derailleur reduces unsprung mass at the rear wheel. This allows the suspension to respond more readily to bumps, potentially improving traction and wheel tracking. Actual improvement depends on the complete suspension design.
  • More balanced handling. Housing the transmission low, near the bottom bracket, centralizes mass instead of carrying it at the rear wheel. That can affect how readily the bike changes direction and reacts to rider input, even if total bike weight does not decrease.
  • Simpler, better-protected components. Integrated eCVT designs eliminate the rear derailleur and multi-speed cassette. No exposed derailleur to strike a rock, no shifting adjustments at the rear wheel, and fewer external drivetrain parts to maintain. Internal transmission service remains a consideration.
  • Smoother, smarter operation. A continuously variable transmission can adjust its ratio automatically to maintain a chosen pedaling cadence, including on steep or changing terrain, without jumping between fixed gears.
  • Chains or belts. A simple single-speed chain remains an option. Compatible belt-drive bikes can also avoid chain lubrication and rust, with less routine cleaning and potentially longer service life. Belts need suitable frame design, tension control, and protection from debris.

Individually, these are meaningful improvements. Together, they could fundamentally change the experience of riding a trail eMTB. The engineering case is compelling, but transmission efficiency, total system weight, long-term reliability, and serviceability still need independent production-bike testing.

Technology worth watching ⌄

Avinox MG

An integrated motor and continuously variable transmission concept. The shift away from a rear derailleur is the important story, not simply peak motor output. Production launch timing and real-world testing remain to be confirmed.

Gobao X1P

Another integrated eCVT approach, with automatic ratio control and support for a simplified chain or belt drivetrain. Early production-bike announcements make this one to follow closely.

How We Rate Bikes

The FindMyBike approach

FindMyBike features a carefully curated collection of exceptional mountain bikes. We don't believe there's one best bike for everyone. Our goal is to find the bike that's best for you.

1

How we evaluate bikes

We combine independent, hands-on reviews of current-generation bikes with manufacturer specifications to assess climbing, descending, handling, and overall ride character.

We compare multiple reviewers whenever possible and update our ratings as new information becomes available.

2

How we find your match

We consider your riding style, terrain, budget, equipment preferences, and priorities to identify the bikes best suited to you. Your Match Score reflects how well a bike fits your needs, not simply how highly we rate its performance.

Value matters too. Your budget determines which builds are eligible. Separately, your bang-for-your-buck preference can raise or lower a bike's match score by comparing the price and estimated equipment level of an eligible build with similarly oriented bikes. Value-focused riders get a stronger adjustment; premium-focused riders get little or none. We don't automatically favor cheaper bikes or reward or penalize brands by name.

Value comparisons are approximate and depend on the build specifications and prices we have verified, so they're one input alongside ride performance and fit.

How Cool Factor is calculated

Cool Factor is an editorial desirability score: brand desirability and prestige 40%, model design and aesthetics 30%, engineering innovation and uniqueness 20%, and enthusiast appeal 10%. Brand desirability reflects current mountain-bike enthusiast reputation rather than price or mainstream popularity. Each current model now has separate, audited editorial design, innovation and enthusiast-appeal ratings. These are informed judgments, not survey-derived numerical measurements or independent review averages. Your personal brand preferences are a separate match-score adjustment.

4
